**Quantum mechanics** is the fundamental physical [theory](Scientific_theory "Scientific theory") that describes the behavior of matter and of light; its unusual characteristics typically occur at and below the scale of [atoms](Atom "Atom"). It is the foundation of all **quantum physics** , which includes [quantum chemistry](Quantum_chemistry "Quantum chemistry"), [quantum biology](Quantum_biology "Quantum biology"), [quantum field theory](Quantum_field_theory "Quantum field theory"), [quantum technology](Quantum_technology "Quantum technology"), and [quantum information science](Quantum_information_science "Quantum information science"). 

Quantum mechanics can describe many systems that [classical physics](Classical_physics "Classical physics") cannot. Classical physics can describe many aspects of nature at an ordinary ([macroscopic](Macroscopic "Macroscopic") and [(optical) microscopic](Microscopic_scale "Microscopic scale")) scale, but is not sufficient for describing them at very small [submicroscopic](Submicroscopic "Submicroscopic") (atomic and [subatomic](Subatomic "Subatomic")) scales. Classical mechanics can be derived from quantum mechanics as an approximation that is valid at ordinary scales. 

**Quantum systems** have [bound](Bound_state "Bound state") states that are [quantized](Quantization_\(physics\) "Quantization \(physics\)") to [discrete values](Discrete_mathematics "Discrete mathematics") of [energy](Energy "Energy"), [momentum](Momentum "Momentum"), [angular momentum](Angular_momentum "Angular momentum"), and other quantities, in contrast to classical systems where these quantities can be measured continuously. Measurements of quantum systems show characteristics of both [particles](Particle "Particle") and [waves](Wave "Wave") ([wave–particle duality](Wave%E2%80%93particle_duality "Wave–particle duality")), and there are limits to how accurately the value of a physical quantity can be predicted prior to its measurement, given a complete set of initial conditions (the [uncertainty principle](Uncertainty_principle "Uncertainty principle")). 

Quantum mechanics arose gradually from theories to explain observations that could not be reconciled with [classical physics](Classical_physics "Classical physics"), such as [Max Planck](Max_Planck "Max Planck")'s solution in 1900 to the [black-body radiation](Black-body_radiation "Black-body radiation") problem, and the correspondence between energy and frequency in [Albert Einstein](Albert_Einstein "Albert Einstein")'s [1905 paper](Annus_Mirabilis_papers#Photoelectric_effect "Annus Mirabilis papers"), which explained the [photoelectric effect](Photoelectric_effect "Photoelectric effect"). These early attempts to understand microscopic phenomena, now known as the "[old quantum theory](Old_quantum_theory "Old quantum theory")", led to the full development of quantum mechanics in the mid-1920s by [Niels Bohr](Niels_Bohr "Niels Bohr"), [Erwin Schrödinger](Erwin_Schr%C3%B6dinger "Erwin Schrödinger"), [Werner Heisenberg](Werner_Heisenberg "Werner Heisenberg"), [Max Born](Max_Born "Max Born"), [Paul Dirac](Paul_Dirac "Paul Dirac") and others. The modern theory is formulated in various [specially developed mathematical formalisms](Mathematical_formulations_of_quantum_mechanics "Mathematical formulations of quantum mechanics"). In one of them, a mathematical entity called the [wave function](Wave_function "Wave function") provides information, in the form of [probability amplitudes](Probability_amplitude "Probability amplitude"), about what measurements of a particle's energy, momentum, and other physical properties may yield.
